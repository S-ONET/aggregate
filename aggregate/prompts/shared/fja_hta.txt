HOW A TASK STATEMENT IS BUILT, AND AT WHAT GRAIN. Two established methods govern every statement here.

FUNCTIONAL JOB ANALYSIS (Fine & Wiley 1971; Fine & Cronshaw 1999): THE STATEMENT'S STRUCTURE.
A task statement answers, in this order and in one sentence:
  1. PERFORMS WHAT ACTION — one concrete action verb, present tense, imperative form, the worker implicit.
     The verb names what an observer would see the worker doing. Carrier and umbrella verbs (manage, handle,
     oversee, ensure, support, perform, coordinate, deal with, be responsible for) name a responsibility, not
     an action; promote the act they carry ("perform inspections" -> "inspect").
  2. TO WHAT OR WHOM — the immediate object: the thing acted on or the party acted with. Specific enough to
     picture, general enough to hold across employers; brand and product names become their generic class.
  3. USING WHAT — the tools, equipment, materials or work aids the source names, when it names them.
  4. ON WHAT INSTRUCTION — the source of direction or the discretion exercised, only when the source states it
     ("per the physician's orders", "within approved budgets").
  5. TO PRODUCE WHAT — the output or result, as a "to ..." clause, when the source states one. The output is
     what the task leaves behind: a record filed, a part fitted, a decision reached, a customer served.
One verb, one object, one output. A sentence that needs two verbs with two different outputs is two tasks.
WORKER-FUNCTION ORIENTATION. Every task relates the worker primarily to one of three things, at a level:
  DATA    — comparing, copying, computing, compiling, analyzing, coordinating, synthesizing.
  PEOPLE  — taking instructions or helping, serving, exchanging information, coaching, persuading,
            consulting, instructing, treating, supervising, negotiating, mentoring.
  THINGS  — handling, feeding or offbearing, tending, manipulating, operating or controlling, driving or
            controlling, precision working, setting up.
Record the orientation (data / people / things) and the level. Two halves of a statement that sit in two
different orientations, or at two different levels, are two tasks: this is the structural form of the
one-duty test.

HIERARCHICAL TASK ANALYSIS (Annett & Duncan 1967; Shepherd 2001): THE GRAIN.
Work decomposes as a hierarchy: JOB -> DUTY (a goal or area of responsibility) -> TASK (a sub-goal with an
identifiable output, accomplished by a plan over its steps) -> STEP (an operation inside a task).
  · A DUTY names a responsibility with no single output: "manage accounts payable", "patient care",
    "quality assurance". It is a heading, not a task.
  · A TASK names one sub-goal with an output a worker could be observed completing: "process vendor invoices
    for payment", "administer medications per the care plan", "run regression tests on each release".
  · A STEP is an operation inside one task, with no output of its own: "enter the invoice number",
    "open the medication cart", "click run".
THE UNIT OF THE INVENTORY IS THE TASK. A duty-level line is kept only as the heading its tasks sit under; a
step-level line is rolled up into the task it serves. STOP RULE (Annett's P x C analogue): decompose a line
only while the pieces have DISTINCT OUTPUTS; where the pieces share one output they are one task, however
many operations it takes. Where a source names only a duty and nothing under it, decompose no further than
the source's own words allow — do not invent the tasks a duty "must" contain.
